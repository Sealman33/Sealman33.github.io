Why do WE love tsunderes?

Ok, I know that this is a pretty crazy title, but hear me out. Tsunderes don’t really make sense as likable characters. Yet, so many people watch shows and fantasize over them. There’s something about this character type that captivates audiences and makes them one of the most popular archetypes in anime. Tsunderes are mean, unreasonable, and act in an entitled, bratty manner. Take Nino from The Quintessential Quintuplets, for example. In season one of the anime, her actions are unreasonable, and she should be a completely unlikable character. She’s insanely mean to Futaro and does everything in her power to make his life miserable, even taking it as far as drugging him. Despite this, she’s arguably the most popular quintuplet — she is my favorite — and many people think that she deserved to win over Yotsuba.

So why is it that Nino and many other tsunderes are fawned over like they are some precious commodity? I believe it comes down to one point: humans are attracted to people who don’t want them, or, more broadly, humans are attracted to things they cannot obtain. We find a sort of beauty in chasing the people who don’t want us, and it makes us want to like them more. We put them on a pedestal and observe them from below as though they are above us. This kind of dynamic makes us more willing to accept their actions and justify poor behavior. It creates a gap in power, and when they do give us a little push, some kind of affection, we cling onto it harder because of its contrast with the previously cold behavior. Sometimes, it can create a cycle where we yearn more and more for this person in a melancholic way that leaves us unsatisfied, with a deep desire for more.

Tsunderes are like cats; their apathy makes them more cute and attractive. The struggle to get their attention is enjoyable in many ways, and we value their affection more because of its scarcity. Personally, I like dogs more, but this analogy serves the point I’m trying to make, so I’ll roll with it.

It’s not all bad though. I think that this idolization of people who don’t reciprocate our feelings can often be a positive emotion for growth. When we look up at someone from below, it motivates us to become better. We want to work harder to impress this person, and it can push us to become better people. We want to live up to our expectations of someone who would be worthy of them. For me, many of my biggest periods of growth occured because of people I looked up to, both romantically and platonically. Moreover, by viewing a person from this perspective, you often don’t notice their flaws and awfully ordinary attributes, allowing them to remain as a dreamy and intangible concept in your mind. This distance between you and the person ensures that you don’t get too close and your image of them doesn’t get shattered by reality.

Well, does this really have anything to do with tsunderes? Maybe? Take it as you will. Maybe I’m just ranting about my unfortunate yearning for people who don’t like me back. But our love for anime often reflects emotions and connections that we have with aspects of our life. Perhaps tsunderes are a reflection of our want for people who feel far ahead of us, people who we’re always watching from behind.

Maybe when she turns around and acts like she doesn’t like you, it makes your heart skip a little faster, making you watch a little longer, hoping that you’ll see the main character feel the satisfaction that you might never feel.
