Takopi's Original Sin, (Not) A Children's Show.

CONTENT WARNING: The show mentioned in this review contains topics of suicide, murder and domestic abuse.
Minor spoilers for Takopi’s Original Sin.

I’m not usually one to go for very dark or “horrory” shows. Ok, to be fair, I am a big believer that media and art are designed to make you feel emotions, whether that’s happiness, sadness, or whatever Solo Leveling’s aura farming is. Yet, many shows try to play into this too hard and just end up being overly edgy or a clear tearjerker trying to make a quick buck. Let’s be clear: Takopi’s Original Sin is not one of those shows. What makes Takopi stand out to me is not necessarily the dark topics that it addresses—since they’re nothing new to anime—but specifically the character of Takopi.

It’s easy to watch a show like Takopi and miss its central meaning, omitting it simply as a show that depicts forms of childhood trauma and suffering (while it does do that, there’s more). The innocent, oblivious, and cheerful alien “tako” that is Takopi is an incongruity that stands out in a show that tackles many serious topics like suicide and domestic abuse. Takopi’s absurdity is meant to slap the viewer back to reality, forcing us to reflect on the actions happening in the show. As humans, we’ve grown desensitized to a lot of things, and we lose the natural curiosity we harbored as kids. Things like suicide, abuse, and bullying—we’ve come to simply accept that they are an inherent part of human existence. But why? Why do we blindly accept such cruel things into our reality without questioning our actions as a society? The primary goal of a character like Takopi is to make us reflect on that. As an alien from “Planet Happy,” Takopi doesn’t have an understanding of human suffering. This makes him question the cruel realities he has to witness through the three main characters. He’s confused and wants to understand why humans harm each other and themselves, while also always giving the benefit of the doubt to everyone he meets. Takopi’s inability to understand why humans harm each other is meant to serve as a wake-up call to the viewer, making them ask themselves the same questions.

Kuze (the main girl) is the opposite of Takopi. If Takopi is a cheerful optimist who always questions why others inflict harm, then Kuze is the desensitized, apathetic, traumatized child who simply accepts that suffering is inevitable in her life. Kuze never once questions why others choose to harm her, as this is the environment she’s always lived in. From a neglectful mother, to a father who has left her, to the children at school who constantly pick on her, her environment has taught her that it is pointless to question the actions of others because she has no control over them. As a child, it’s easy to feel powerless—to feel like our lives are controlled by others—and to a degree, it’s true.

When it came to the struggles of the main characters, what resonated with me the most were many of the little troubles they faced. Thankfully, most of us have not had to go through the same horrors as the main characters, so it may be hard for us to relate to their major issues. Take Azuma, for example. He was one of my favorite characters in the show. I may not know what it feels like to be deprived of food for getting a 95 on a test (a score that I would gladly take); however, I understand what it can feel like to believe that the only way to get acknowledgment from your parents is through good grades. Although Azuma as a character is a trope taken to the extreme, many of his internal struggles are both realistic and relatable for many viewers, myself included. It’s moments like when Azuma is willing to go to any length for Kuze because he feels that she’s the only person who acknowledges his existence that touched me the deepest, because this toxic form of yearning is something I can absolutely understand.

One of the main criticisms I’ve seen regarding the show is that it is overly miserable and that the plot ignores too many aspects of reality, breaking immersion. While I understand where this is coming from—since I also had moments where I wondered, “Why is no police officer arresting this child wanted for murder?”—I think this aspect is not a plot hole but an intentional choice by the author. At an aggregate level, ignoring its intricacies, Takopi wants to create as much of an oxymoron as possible by sticking a joyful octopus in the middle of the most cruel childhood trauma imaginable. I mean, there’s a fucking magic octopus—I don’t think the primary objective of the show is to portray suffering through strict realism.

In the end, Takopi’s Original Sin knows what it wants to do and does it very well. It creates an extremely uncomfortable atmosphere by blending raw, unjustified misery inflicted on children with the cheerful tone of a children’s anime. We have a natural tendency to find this kind of combination almost repulsive, as it confuses our emotions and leaves us unsure of how we should feel. Takopi’s extreme depictions of childhood suffering are sometimes amplified versions of real scenarios, showing how overwhelming these situations can feel when you’re trapped in them. The emotions and suffering in Takopi are undoubtedly going to resonate with many viewers, making them reflect on moments of hardship from their own childhoods. While this show definitely hurt me, I’m glad I watched it, because it reminded me that anime can do more than just make me laugh and entertain me. Anime can make me sad, anime can make me reflect, and anime can hurt—but I’m grateful that I get to experience all of these emotions through this form of art.
